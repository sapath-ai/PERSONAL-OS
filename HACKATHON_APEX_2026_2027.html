UPDATE 01 · Foundation

Hackathon Competition Fundamentals

Before learning to optimize an event, learn what the competition actually is. This module takes you from “I know people build projects at hackathons” to being able to model the rules, incentives, clock, evaluation surface, submission contract, and win condition of a competition.

Outcome: by the end of Update 01, you should be able to open an unfamiliar competition page and produce a one-page Competition Blueprint covering format, eligibility, timeline, judging, submission, team strategy, technical constraints, evidence requirements, and the exact pathway from idea → build → evaluation.
01 · WHAT IS BEING COMPETED ON?

Competition model

Classify the event before preparing for it: build-and-pitch, sponsor challenge, research/technical, open-source contribution, hardware/embedded, benchmark/leaderboard, or hybrid. The model determines what “winning” can even mean.

02 · THE EVALUATION SURFACE

Judging vs measurement

Separate subjective judging from objective scoring. Learn the difference between rubrics, leaderboards, benchmarks, public votes, demos, interviews, and judge Q&A—and identify which one dominates the result.

03 · THE CLOCK

Time economics

Understand idea time, setup time, integration time, validation time, submission time, demo rehearsal, and contingency. A hackathon is a constrained optimization problem, not a normal software sprint.

04 · THE CONTRACT

Eligibility & submission

Read age/student/team rules, technology restrictions, source requirements, deployment expectations, originality clauses, sponsor/API requirements, asset ownership, deadlines, and required submission artifacts.

05 · THE BUILD

What counts as a project?

Distinguish prototype, functional MVP, proof-of-concept, research artifact, hardware prototype, open-source contribution, trading strategy, and benchmark submission. Match the build type to the competition archetype.

06 · THE TEAM

Human resource model

Understand individual vs team events, team-size constraints, role specialization, dependency chains, parallel work, review ownership, integration ownership, and the cost of coordination under a hard deadline.

07 · THE JUDGE

Judge journey

Model what a judge actually encounters: first impression, problem framing, evidence, live demo, technical questioning, failure cases, and final comparison against other teams. The judging journey is part of the product.

08 · THE PROOF

Evidence hierarchy

Learn the difference between saying, showing, measuring, reproducing, and independently validating a claim. Treat demos, metrics, benchmarks, logs, hardware measurements, citations, and source code as different evidence types.

09 · THE TRADE-OFF

Scope control

Every feature consumes engineering hours and increases integration risk. Learn to rank work by expected competitive value, technical dependency, proof cost, and failure risk rather than by excitement.

10 · THE DIFFERENTIATOR

Why this project?

Separate novelty of the problem, novelty of the mechanism, novelty of the implementation, quality of execution, and presentation novelty. “Uses AI” is not a differentiator by itself.

11 · THE SUBMISSION

Artifact completeness

Recognize the submission as a scored artifact: title, one-line pitch, problem, solution, architecture, demo, technical explanation, screenshots/video, repository, setup instructions, and prize-specific evidence where applicable.

12 · THE OUTCOME

Winning is multi-objective

Optimize for the actual objective: podium placement, sponsor prize, leaderboard rank, adoption, open-source impact, research credibility, portfolio signal, or some combination. Do not confuse “finished” with “won.”

RULE HIERARCHY

When the competition rules conflict

Beginners often read rules as one flat document. Treat them as a precedence stack so a prize description never accidentally overrides a disqualification rule or official clarification.

01 · Official rulesEligibility, disqualifiers, deadlines, ownership and legal constraints.
02 · Official clarificationsOrganizer updates, FAQs, amendments and announcement-channel corrections.
03 · Track / prize rulesSpecific sponsor technology, category, platform or judging requirements.
04 · Submission formFields, upload limits, links, formats and evidence requested at submission.
05 · Community chatterUseful for interpretation and discovery, never the authority for compliance.
COMPETITION-PAGE INTERROGATION DRILL

Ask these questions before you ever choose an idea

What can disqualify us?List prohibited technologies, prior work restrictions, eligibility, IP, team-size and deadline failures.
What is actually scored?Separate mandatory gates from scored dimensions and measurable leaderboard variables.
Who evaluates us?Identify organizers, technical judges, sponsors, community voting, panels or automated scoring.
What must be demonstrated?Define the minimum live path, recordings, benchmark evidence, code, hardware and documentation required.
What changes at the track level?Record sponsor APIs, required platforms, special criteria and prize-specific submission requirements.
What happens after submission?Map screening, finalist selection, demo slots, Q&A, leaderboard windows, judging and announcements.
UPDATE 02 · Landscape

Global Hackathon Landscape & Competition Taxonomy

Do not treat every “hackathon” as the same game. First classify the competition by what is being evaluated, how results are produced, where differentiation comes from, and what evidence survives judging.

Core model: every competition is a tuple of format × evaluation mechanism × time horizon × ecosystem × evidence standard × reward structure. Your preparation strategy is derived from that tuple.

Build + Pitch

Classic weekend hackathon.

  • Prototype speed
  • Working vertical slice

  • Demo reliability

  • Mandatory or strategic API usage

  • Baseline comparison

  • Measured performance

Open Source

  • Accepted code/design/docs
  • Commit evidence
  • Licensing
  • Community usefulness

Protocol / Web3

Composable infrastructure and ecosystem integration.

  • Smart contracts / protocols
  • Wallet / identity flows
  • Security
  • On-chain proof
TYPE 07

Benchmark / Leaderboard

Objective measured performance is the battlefield.

  • Defined metric
  • Baseline
  • Optimization loop
  • Reproducible result
TYPE 08

Quant / Trading

Research, execution and risk are evaluated quantitatively.

  • Backtest discipline
  • Out-of-sample testing
  • Risk metrics
  • Simulation / live result
TYPE 09

Long-Horizon Build

Days/weeks instead of one weekend.

  • Development trajectory
  • Iteration evidence
  • Documentation quality
  • Final-state polish
TYPE 10

Challenge / Case

Organized around a fixed business or societal problem.

  • Problem framing
  • Constraint compliance
  • Feasibility
  • Solution defensibility
TYPE 11

Community / Festival

Recurring events that mix learning, building and networking.

  • Technical drills
  • Community participation
  • Mini-projects
  • Builder network
TYPE 12

Hybrid

Multiple evaluation modes in one competition.

  • Automated score + judges
  • Demo + benchmark
  • Sponsor prize + grand prize
  • Multiple submission surfaces
COMPETITION SELECTION RULE

Classify first. Then decide whether to compete.

For every event, record six fields: what is ranked, who ranks it, the time horizon, the evidence standard, the ecosystem leverage, and the reward surface. An event can then be compared against your current capability, travel/eligibility constraints, domain fit, and expected learning value.

UPDATE 04 · Evaluation Science

Judge Psychology & Evaluation Science

A strong project can still lose if its value, evidence, or technical depth is difficult to detect under judging conditions. This module trains you to engineer the judge's path from first impression to final score without relying on manipulation.

Evidence principle: different competitions expose judges to different scoring surfaces. Hack the North explicitly evaluates WOW factor, technical ability, originality, and design; ETHGlobal-style judging evaluates technicality, originality, practicality, usability, and WOW factor; NASA Space Apps uses staged judging and separate award lenses. The job is therefore to make valid evidence easy to perceive—not to game the judge. Hack the North · ETHGlobal · NASA Space Apps
Cognition evidence: Cognitive Load Theory models working-memory capacity as limited and supports reducing unnecessary processing demands; contemporary multimedia-learning reviews emphasize limited capacity, active processing, and coherent integration of verbal and visual information. Apex uses these findings as a design rationale for concise, structured judge communication—not as a claim that any single presentation trick guarantees higher scores. · Sweller et al. review · Mayer review
01 · ATTENTION

First-impression design

Control what the judge understands in the first 5–15 seconds: project name, user, problem, and visible consequence. Remove visual ambiguity before adding visual polish.

02 · COGNITIVE LOAD

Compress the explanation

Reduce working-memory burden with familiar metaphors, one-line positioning, progressive disclosure, and diagrams that show causality rather than decoration.

03 · EVIDENCE

Claims need proof

Rank evidence from assertion → screenshot → live behavior → metric → benchmark → reproducible artifact. Make the strongest valid evidence appear earliest.

04 · COMPARISON

Make the delta visible

Judges compare projects against each other. Show before/after, baseline/ours, manual/automated, centralized/federated, cloud/edge, or other honest contrast.

05 · MEMORABILITY

One anchor moment

Give the judge one concrete moment to remember: a physical action, surprising transformation, compelling visualization, measurable jump, or unusually elegant workflow.

06 · RUBRIC MAPPING

Make criteria legible

Every major judging criterion should have visible evidence. Never make judges infer how a feature satisfies a criterion.

07 · TIME COMPRESSION

Design for partial attention

Prepare 30-second, 60-second, 2-minute, and deep-dive versions. The project must remain coherent when the judge has less time than expected.

08 · PANEL VARIANCE

Survive different judges

Technical and non-technical judges can value different evidence. Use a layered explanation: human outcome first, mechanism second, implementation depth third.

09 · Q&A

Defense under pressure

Answer the question asked, then support it with architecture, measurement, trade-offs, or failure evidence. Never bluff when a limitation is real.

10 · CREDIBILITY

Calibrated claims

Separate “we demonstrated,” “we measured,” “we estimate,” and “we intend.” Overclaiming can destroy trust faster than a missing feature.

11 · BIAS CONTROL

Engineer for fairness

Do not rely on hype, jargon, status, or theatrics alone. Make the same core evidence discoverable even when the judge starts skeptical.

12 · FINAL COMPARISON

Give the judge a reason

End with the single strongest defensible reason this project should outrank alternatives under the event's actual scoring surface.

Spend tokens where they change outcomes

05 · RAG DECISION

Earn the right to retrieve

Choose plain prompting, retrieval, fine-tuning, or a deterministic lookup according to the task. RAG is justified only when grounding against changing or private information materially improves the result.

06 · AGENT ORCHESTRATION

One reliable loop before a swarm

Start with a narrow agent loop: observe → decide → act → verify. Add planning, delegation, or multi-agent coordination only when a single loop demonstrably fails the task.

07 · MODEL ROUTING

Match model power to task value

Route trivial transformations to cheaper/faster models and reserve stronger models for reasoning-heavy steps. Track quality, latency, token cost, and failure rate by route.

08 · FALLBACK ENGINEERING

Live AI must never be the only path

Build cached results, deterministic rules, seeded fixtures, or recorded outputs for demo-critical flows. The fallback should preserve the user-visible story when model/API availability collapses.

09 · HUMAN REVIEW

Place verification at the expensive boundaries

Human checkpoints belong where mistakes are costly: irreversible actions, external communications, security-sensitive changes, clinical or financial claims, and final releases.

10 · AI COST / LATENCY

Treat inference as a production dependency

Measure first-token latency, total latency, tokens, retries, failure rate, and per-demo cost. A “smarter” model that breaks the judge's interaction window is not an improvement.

UPDATE 12 · Data, ML & Proof

Data, ML, Evaluation & Proof

Turn data and models into judge-verifiable evidence. This module treats dataset quality, baselines, metrics, error analysis, reproducibility, and resource measurements as first-class engineering components rather than a final reporting exercise.

Evaluation law: no headline claim without a defined test population, leakage-safe protocol, baseline, metric rationale, uncertainty/limitations, and inspectable failure cases. A higher number is useless if the protocol is weak.
01 · DATA CONTRACT

Know exactly what the model consumes

Define schema, labels, units, missingness, provenance, licensing/usage constraints, normalization, and expected distributions before model training.

02 · SPLIT HYGIENE

Protect the evaluation from leakage

Choose train/validation/test partitions according to the real deployment unit: person, patient, device, organization, time window, geography, or session.

03 · BASELINE

Beat something defensible

Build the simplest credible baseline first. Record its configuration, runtime, evaluation protocol, and result so your improvement has meaning.

04 · METRIC SELECTION

Match the metric to the failure cost

Choose metrics according to the actual decision. Accuracy alone can hide class imbalance; latency, calibration, recall, precision, robustness, or resource use may matter more.

05 · ERROR ANALYSIS

Study what the model gets wrong

Slice errors by class, subgroup, input quality, difficulty, operating condition, and failure mechanism. One useful error table can reveal more than another decimal place.

06 · ABLATION

Prove which component creates the gain

Remove or disable a meaningful component and rerun the evaluation. This prevents a complex system from claiming credit for an improvement caused elsewhere.

07 · THRESHOLD / CALIBRATION

Make decisions explicit

Show how thresholds, confidence scores, calibration, or ranking cutoffs change outcomes. A probability output is not automatically a trustworthy decision.

08 · ROBUSTNESS

Test beyond the happy distribution

Probe noise, missing data, distribution shift, adversarial or malformed inputs, boundary cases, and resource-constrained conditions appropriate to the task.

09 · RESOURCE PROFILE

Measure the system, not just the score

Record training/inference time, memory, throughput, model size, communication cost, energy or power when relevant, and monetary cost where relevant.

10 · REPRODUCIBILITY

Make the result rerunnable

Pin versions, save seeds, store configs, preserve dataset identifiers/splits, log experiments, capture code revision, and record the exact command needed to reproduce the headline result.

UPDATE 14 · Reliability & Demo Resilience

Reliability, Security & Demo-Resilience Engineering

Build for the environment you actually have: unreliable networks, expired sessions, missing devices, rate limits, malformed inputs, partial service failure, noisy judges, and almost no recovery time. Reliability is not polish; it is competitive risk control.

Resilience law: every judge-critical flow needs a known dependency set, failure behavior, fallback path, recovery trigger, and rehearsal record. A system that works only when everything goes right is not demo-ready.
01 · FAILURE MODE ANALYSIS

Enumerate before you experience

Map dependency, input, state, security, deployment, environment, and human-error failure modes. Rank them by probability × judge impact × recovery difficulty.

02 · GRACEFUL DEGRADATION

Lose optional capability, not the story

Define degraded modes where non-core services fail without destroying the judge-visible path. Label degraded output honestly.

03 · TIMEOUTS & RETRIES

Bound every wait

Every networked or asynchronous dependency gets explicit timeout, retry count, backoff, and terminal behavior. Never let the UI hang indefinitely.

04 · IDEMPOTENCY

Assume duplicated actions

Make critical mutations safe to retry or explicitly detect duplicates. Repeated clicks, browser refreshes, and replayed webhooks must not corrupt state.

05 · SECURITY BASELINES

Protect the demo's trust boundary

Verify authorization, secret handling, input validation, dependency trust, least privilege, and safe logging. Never expose credentials to the browser, repository, or demo console.

06 · SECRETS & CONFIG

Separate code from event-specific configuration

Use environment variables or secret stores appropriately, rotate compromised credentials, and prepare a no-secret local/demo mode.

07 · OBSERVABILITY

Diagnose in seconds, not archaeology

Use correlation IDs, structured errors, dependency timing, health signals, and concise logs so a failure can be localized during judging.

08 · HEALTH & SMOKE TESTS

Detect broken infrastructure before the demo

Create a one-command smoke test for runtime, critical services, data fixtures, authentication, model/API reachability, and the golden path.

09 · RECOVERY & ROLLBACK

Make the last known-good state reachable

Tag known-good commits, preserve deployable artifacts, document rollback steps, and define when to stop debugging and revert.

10 · ADVERSARIAL DEMO REHEARSAL

Rehearse under hostile conditions

Test dead Wi-Fi, slow APIs, wrong input, expired auth, cold starts, projector/browser differences, and an interrupted presenter.

11 · INCIDENT COMMUNICATION

Stay judgeable during failure

Prepare a 10-second failure script: identify the issue, switch to fallback, preserve the story, and explain the engineering trade-off without panic.

12 · RESILIENCE BUDGET

Spend effort where failure is expensive

Do not harden everything equally. Protect the golden path, proof metric, critical dependencies, security boundary, and recovery path first.

Control downside explicitly

Team law: optimize for clear ownership + fast decisions + low integration friction. Collaboration is not “everyone touches everything”; it is a deliberately designed interface between specialists.

Integrate deliberately

Reach judge-visible value early

Spend complexity where it earns points

Collect proof continuously

Force composition before polish

Require clean-environment execution of the real judge path before allowing broad feature expansion.

09 · RECOVERY

Design for degraded operation

10 · SUBMISSION CONTROL

11 · DEMO CONTROL

Run one deterministic judge path

12 · INCIDENT COMMAND

Assign the recovery authority

13 · ENERGY / HUMAN FACTOR

Protect performance under the clock

Schedule breaks, food, hydration, sleep where possible, and rotation of high-cognition tasks. Avoid placing all final-state reasoning on one exhausted person.

14 · POST-EVENT MEMORY

Convert the event into system updates

UPDATE 23 · Pitch, Demo, README, Video & Judge Defense

Convert the engineering work into judge-readable evidence. The final deliverable is a coherent proof narrative: the right claim, the right artifact, the right demo moment, and a defensible answer when challenged.

Judge-proof law: every important statement follows Claim → Context → Evidence → Demonstration → Boundary → Answer. Never use the pitch to hide missing engineering; use it to make the engineering legible.
01 · NARRATIVE ARCHITECTURE

Build one causal story

Problem → consequence → intervention → mechanism → measured result → user value. Each sentence should set up the next proof artifact.

02 · FIRST-30-SECONDS

Win orientation immediately

State the user, pain, outcome, and one differentiator before technology names. The judge should know what to look for before the demo begins.

03 · DEMO SCRIPT

Use a deterministic judge path

Define the exact clicks, seeded data, expected outputs, transition points, and backup invocation. Eliminate exploratory wandering.

04 · PROOF MOMENTS

Expose difficult-to-fake evidence

Show a metric, trace, benchmark, architecture boundary, hardware reading, clinical workflow, or other artifact that directly supports the strongest claim.

05 · TECHNICAL DEEP-DIVE

Earn the “why is this hard?” answer

Prepare one subsystem whose constraints, mechanism, trade-off, and measured improvement can be explained without a lecture on the entire stack.

06 · README ENGINEERING

Make the repository judge-runnable

07 · VIDEO CONSTRAINT

Compress the proof into a backup artifact

Record the same core journey with visible outcome, technical proof, and captioned context. The video is insurance and an asynchronous evidence channel.

08 · RUBRIC MAPPING

Make scoring legible

For every criterion, identify the exact slide, demo moment, repository artifact, or measurement that proves it. Do not rely on judge inference.

09 · Q&A TRIAGE

Answer with evidence before detail

Use Answer → Evidence → Boundary → Next-step structure. Lead with the conclusion, then give the proof, then state the limitation.

10 · COMPARATIVE DEFENSE

Explain why this beats plausible alternatives

Know the strongest baseline, incumbent, simpler implementation, and competing approach. Defend the design choice without denigrating alternatives.

11 · CLAIM CALIBRATION

Separate observed from inferred

Use precise language for prototype evidence, simulated results, benchmark gains, user feedback, clinical claims, and future potential.

12 · LIVE RECOVERY

Recover without losing judge trust

Define the exact sentence, fallback path, and evidence artifact used after an API failure, malformed output, deployment problem, or demo interruption.

13 · VISUAL INFORMATION DESIGN

Show the right thing at the right time

Use one visual hierarchy for problem, mechanism, result, and architecture. Remove screenshots and diagrams that do not change a judging decision.

14 · FINAL JUDGE DEFENSE

Close with one defensible reason to win

End on the sharpest combination of user value, technical differentiation, and concrete proof—not a generic “thank you” or feature list.

The event is not the end of the experiment. The result is evidence about the competition system, the project, the team, and the assumptions behind both.

01 · OUTCOME DECOMPOSITION

Separate win, loss, and placement from causality

Turn comments into structured data

Audit the clock

Separate variance from skill

Change the system deliberately
