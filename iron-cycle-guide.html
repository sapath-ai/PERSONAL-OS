01 — Read This First

Before You Start

If you've never followed a structured program before, welcome — this section covers the handful of things that matter more than any single exercise: how to pick a starting weight, how to breathe and brace, how much rest to take, and how to tell good soreness from a warning sign.

Picking Your Starting Weight

For every new exercise, start lighter than you think you need to. Run through the first set at roughly half of what feels challenging, check your form, then add weight on the following sets until the last few reps of a set feel genuinely difficult without breaking form. It's far easier to add weight next session than to walk back a knee, shoulder, or lower-back tweak from starting too heavy.

Rule of thumbYou should always feel like you had 1–2 more reps left in the tank on your working sets during your first few weeks. That margin is what keeps a beginner training consistently instead of getting sidelined.

Rest Between Sets

Rest 60–90 seconds between sets of an exercise, and 90 seconds–3 minutes on heavier compound lifts like squats, presses, and deadlifts once you're loading them up. Undershooting rest early on just means worse form and fewer quality reps — you'll shorten these gaps naturally as the Progression section below describes.

Breathing & Bracing

On most lifts: inhale on the lowering (eccentric) part of the movement, exhale through the sticking point as you lift. Before a heavy rep, take a breath and gently brace your core like you're about to take a light punch to the stomach — this stabilizes your spine, especially on squats and rows.

Soreness vs. Pain — Know the Difference

Normal Soreness

A dull, general ache across a muscle you trained, usually peaking 24–48 hours later (DOMS) and fading within a few days. It's fine to train through this — light movement often helps it clear faster.

Stop & Reassess

Sharp, localized, or joint-specific pain, pain that worsens mid-set, or anything that lingers well past a week is your body's flag to back off, drop the load, and check in with a physiotherapist or doctor if it doesn't settle.

The original program principle to "push through soreness" refers to ordinary muscle fatigue and DOMS — not to joint pain or sharp discomfort. When in doubt, treat pain as information, not an obstacle.

02 — Every Session Starts Here

Warmup & Form Basics

A rushed warmup is one of the most common beginner mistakes. Give it five to eight minutes — it's what lets you lift the weights in this program safely.

Raise your heart rate (2–3 min)

Light jogging in place, jumping jacks, or skipping rope. The goal is a light sweat, not fatigue.

Dynamic mobility (2–3 min)

Arm circles, leg swings, hip openers, and bodyweight squats — move the joints you're about to load through their full range.

Ramp-up sets (1–2 sets per lift)

Before your first working set of a compound lift (bench, squat, deadlift variant, overhead press), do 1–2 light sets at roughly 40–60% of your working weight to groove the movement pattern.

Form Cues That Apply Almost Everywhere

  • Full range of motion beats heavier weight with a shortened range — you'll build more strength and mobility.
  • Controlled tempo — roughly 2 seconds down, 1 second up. Momentum-swinging a weight up removes tension from the muscle you're trying to train.
  • Neutral spine on rows, deadlift variants, and squats — think "proud chest," not a rounded back.
  • Squat form: keep your back straight and aim to go below parallel as your mobility allows — depth comes with practice, don't force it on day one.
03 — The Structure

How The Cycle Works

Iron Cycle is not tied to calendar days. You move through seven sessions in a fixed order, at whatever pace your schedule and recovery allow, then the cycle loops back to the start:

A1→ C1→ A2→ C2→ B1→ C3→ B2→ (loops to A1)

Strength sessions (A1, A2, B1, B2) are upper-body focused with some leg work woven in; cardio sessions (C1, C2, C3) are identical in structure — a morning jog toward a step target, plus a short core circuit. Rest whenever you need to between sessions; the cycle waits for you.

05 — Reference

Cardio Rules

  • No HIIT — keep it low-impact.
  • 150 minutes of cardio per week, total.
  • Do cardio on rest days, or split strength (AM) and cardio (PM) with a gap between.
  • Mix more than one type of cardio per week — do the one you enjoy.
  • Refeed after cardio to prevent catabolism.
  • Add ankle weights / a weighted bag to jogging once it feels easy.
06 — The Long Game

When To Progress

Progressive overload — gradually asking more of your muscles over weeks and months — is the single biggest driver of results in this program. Here's the practical timeline for how and when to increase weight, reps, and cardio volume. All weights are tracked in kilograms (kg).

Weightevery 1–2 weeks
Once you complete every set of an exercise at the top of its rep range, with solid form, for two sessions in a row, add weight the next time you see that exercise. Typical increase: +1–2 kg for upper-body lifts, +2–4 kg for lower-body lifts.
Repsevery session
Staying at the same weight? Add 1–2 reps per set each session until every set reaches the top of the range — that's your signal to increase weight and drop back to the bottom of the range next time.
Distance / Stepsevery 1–2 weeks
Raise weekly cardio volume (steps, minutes, or distance) by no more than about 10% at a time. Once your normal jog feels easy at the current distance, layer on ankle weights or a weighted bag before pushing distance further.
Deloadevery 4–6 weeks
Every 4–6 weeks, take one lighter lap through the cycle — same reps, roughly 10% less weight and easier cardio — so joints and connective tissue catch up before you keep pushing loads.

Also decrease rest between sets over timeAs your work capacity improves, gradually trim rest periods toward the lower end of the 60–90 second range on hypertrophy work — it's another lever for progressive overload beyond just adding weight or reps.

07 — Fuel & Recovery

Nutrition & Recovery Basics

Training creates the stimulus; food and sleep are what actually let your body adapt and grow stronger. A beginner who eats and sleeps consistently will out-progress one who trains harder but neglects both.

Protein

Aim for roughly 1.6–2.2g of protein per kg of bodyweight per day, spread across meals, to support muscle repair between sessions.

Hydration

Drink water throughout the day, not just around workouts — mild dehydration alone can make sessions feel noticeably harder.

Sleep

Target 7–9 hours a night. Most of your recovery and adaptation happens during sleep, not during the workout itself.

Energy Balance

A slight calorie surplus generally supports muscle gain; a slight deficit supports fat loss. Either way, keep protein and training consistent — the number on the scale is a slow-moving average, not a daily verdict.

Refeeding After Cardio

On cardio days, eat a meal with carbohydrates and protein within a couple of hours after your session to replenish glycogen and reduce muscle breakdown — this is the "refeed after cardio to prevent catabolism" rule from the Cardio Rules section, in plain terms.

09 — Terms Worth Knowing

Glossary

Set / Rep

A "rep" is one full repetition of an exercise. A "set" is a group of consecutive reps done without resting, e.g. "3 × 8–15" means 3 sets of 8 to 15 reps each.

Progressive Overload

Gradually increasing the demand placed on a muscle over time — via more weight, more reps, more sets, or less rest — so it has a reason to keep adapting.

DOMS

Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness — the normal ache that shows up 24–48 hours after training a muscle, especially with new or unfamiliar exercises.

Compound vs. Isolation

Compound lifts (bench press, squats, rows) move multiple joints and muscle groups at once. Isolation lifts (curls, lateral raises) target one muscle group through a single joint.

Deload

A planned, temporary reduction in training weight or volume — usually every 4–6 weeks — to let the body recover fully before continuing to push loads upward.

Low-Impact Cardio

Cardio that avoids hard, jarring movements — brisk walking, jogging at a conversational pace, cycling, swimming, or rowing — as opposed to HIIT or explosive plyometric training.
